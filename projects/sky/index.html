Numerical Linear Algebra Software in the AI Era: Present and Future

Companion page for our poster at the Sky Computing Lab End-of-Project meeting (UC Berkeley, October 21, 2026).

This page accompanies our poster at the Sky Computing Lab End-of-Project meeting at UC Berkeley (October 21, 2026). The poster has two halves. The left half is about RandBLAS and RandLAPACK, our open-source libraries for randomized numerical linear algebra. The right half is about VERITAS, a proposed framework for testing and certifying numerical linear algebra kernels, and its companion formalization project VSCL. RandBLAS and RandLAPACK

Randomized numerical linear algebra (RandNLA) uses randomness as a computational resource [3]. A sketching operator compresses a matrix into a far smaller one that keeps the original's geometry (norms, angles, leading subspaces) with high probability, so the expensive step of an algorithm runs on a smaller problem; algorithms built this way need fewer passes over the data and are often more robust than their classical counterparts. RandBLAS (release 1.1.0) implements the sketching operators, dense and sparse, and their products behind a BLAS-style API, with the aim of becoming a standard that hardware vendors implement; every random number comes from a counter-based generator [8], so draws are reproducible whatever the thread count. RandLAPACK is the platform for implementing, testing and benchmarking RandNLA methods on top of it: randomized QR with pivoting (BQRRP [1], CQRRPT [2], HQRRP [22]), tall and Q-less QR (CQRRT, CQRRTO [9], CholQR, shifted CholQR3 [23]), low-rank approximation (RSVD [20], ABRIK), symmetric eigenvalue methods (REVD2 [37], RPCholesky [21], Nyström [3]), least squares and kernel ridge regression (KRILL [24], Blendenpik [25], LSQR [26]), matrix functions and traces (funNyström++ [7], Lanczos), and the building blocks beneath them.

The numbers on the poster. The 100x and 55% tiles and the chart come from the BQRRP paper's benchmark data [1]: a dual AMD Zen4c CPU against LAPACK's pivoted QR, and an NVIDIA H100 against cuSOLVER's unpivoted QR. The 10x tile is our funNyström++ condition-number sweep [7]. The 1700x tile is from our June 2025 RandLAPACK report: RPCholesky [21] in RandLAPACK against scikit-learn's [32] kernel PCA on the codrna dataset (RBF kernel, bandwidth 3, eigenvector identities verified to 1e-8; RandLAPACK with p = 4 against scikit-learn with p = 2 as in the source slide) at 100,000 points on an Apple M2 Max, with 3 GB of RAM at 240,000 points.

Installing and calling the libraries

Install

  • One-command installers on Linux, macOS and Windows: see RandLAPACK's INSTALL.md (installs RandBLAS as well); more than 330 CI tests run on all three.
  • Vendor BLAS and LAPACK through BLAS++ and LAPACK++ [34]: tested with OpenBLAS [38], Intel MKL [39], Apple Accelerate [40] and AMD AOCL [41] (BLIS [35] and libflame [36]); the wrappers also support Cray LibSci [42] and IBM ESSL [43].
  • RandLAPACK's 1.0 release candidate targets November 2026.

Call

  • C++ at the core; MATLAB (MEX) and Python bindings (MATLAB covers BQRRP and funNyström++, Python is a BQRRP proof of concept).
  • GPUs: RandLAPACK runs BQRRP and CQRRPT on NVIDIA CUDA; RandBLAS CUDA support is in development.
  • Questions and development: the RandLAPACK Discord server.

The contract

The centre of the ring names what the two halves share. A kernel's contract is what it promises (an error bound, a success probability, its behaviour on exceptions) and what it assumes (the arithmetic and its precision, the hardware and the BLAS beneath it, the inputs). The libraries on the left ship kernels with stated contracts; VERITAS tests a kernel against its contract; VSCL proves the bounds the contracts cite.

VERITAS

VERITAS (Verification Engine for Reliable and Intelligent Testing of Algebra Systems) is a proposed framework for testing and certifying numerical kernels, whoever or whatever wrote them. For fifty years numerical linear algebra meant expert-written, standardized libraries, trusted through test suites and stability proofs. Generative AI now makes a kernel nearly free to produce and expensive to trust: it passes its developer's tests and fails silently on hard inputs, in reduced precision, or on other hardware.

The motivation is the audit shown on the poster. In round 2 of a 2026 GPU fast-QR competition [4], the top five kernels run 87x to 107x faster than cuSOLVER (the organizers' reference) and pass all 19 scored cases; on about 1,050 legal inputs from the organizers' own generator, run through the unchanged checker, every one of them returns wrong factorizations (157, 46, 330, 3 and 360 times). The rank-4 kernel fails only 3 times because it detects hard inputs and falls back to an exact routine. Counting round 1, 10 of 10 top kernels break. In the Cholesky factorization [44] and eigendecomposition [45] follow-ups, 7 of 10 are broken; two of the rest hold only because they fall back to an exact method, and the last fails only in long input sequences. The adversarial testing harness behind those audits exists today; everything else below is proposed.

The proposed design has five stages over one archetype catalog. A kernel comes in as C++ or Python source code, whoever or whatever wrote it. A model recognizes which archetype it implements and writes the adapter; sandboxed probe runs confirm or reject the label; an adversarial sweep, seeded and sandboxed on the kernel's own hardware, measures error against the bound; a signed certificate records hashes, setup and a trust grade. The catalog defines 28 archetypes, informed by the standard BLAS and LAPACK routines that RandBLAS and RandLAPACK call (BLAS 1 to 3, LAPACK factorizations and solvers, sparse kernels, sketching); each carries a contract (error bound, threshold, input generators), and failing cases refine the catalog.

The certificate records the assumptions a kernel was tested under (sizes, data range, precision, exceptions, hardware) and the bound it met, both taken from the archetype's contract: the error bound proved in VSCL [6], a threshold and input generators. Kernels are tested as black boxes, so the tests must resist gaming, through secret seeds, mixed inputs and adversarial search. Related: the proposed consistent exception handling for the BLAS and LAPACK and the IEEE P3109 draft standard for low-precision arithmetic.

VSCL

The Verified Scientific Computing Library (VSCL) [6] puts numerical analysis in Lean 4 [33]; its formalized results are the basis of VERITAS tests, proved theorems rather than citations. The library holds 1.6 million nonblank lines of Lean 4 on top of Mathlib (its README, 2026-10-19); on the 2026-10-06 snapshot it had 40,478 theorem and lemma statements, with 0 sorry and 0 axiom declarations; Mathlib [19] itself has 13 theorem lines on numerical analysis among about 182,000.

Texts. Formalized: all 28 chapters of Higham [10]; LeVeque [11] (chapters 1 to 3); Vershynin [12] (chapters 1 and 2); the Drineas and Mahoney RandNLA case study [13]. In progress: Nocedal and Wright [14], Bhatia [15]. Next: Greenbaum [16], Saad [17], Boyd [18]. Proved today: backward-error bounds for summation, triangular solves, Householder QR and Cholesky; error bounds for Drineas-Mahoney sampling. How it is built: a book-formalization skill drives coding agents chapter by chapter (extract, inventory, prove in Lean 4, audit faithfulness, publish); open-weight provers are under test. On 10 stability-proof tasks, an agent with an early version of the library proved 6.1x faster with 5.4x shorter proofs. Next targets: Lean theorems carrying a building block's error bound up to LU, QR and eigensolver bounds; bounds below 16 bits, first for IEEE P3109 [31] reductions; the MR³ SVD [30].
